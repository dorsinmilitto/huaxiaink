A praying mantis can strike faster than the human eye can track. **Praying Mantis Kung Fu** (螳螂拳, *Tanglangquan*) was designed to do the same — and it has been doing it for over 400 years.

## Born in Shandong

According to legend, a martial artist named Wang Lang observed a praying mantis defeating a much larger cicada during the late [Ming Dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ming_dynasty). Fascinated by the insect's efficiency, Wang spent years studying mantis behavior and codified its movements into a complete fighting system in [Shandong Province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shandong).

The style spread rapidly across northern China and spawned numerous sub-styles. The two major branches are **Seven-Star Praying Mantis** (七星螳螂拳), emphasizing long-range power, and **Six-Harmony Praying Mantis** (六合螳螂拳), focusing on close-quarters combat. Other notable variants include Taiji Mantis, Plum Blossom Mantis, and [Wudang Mantis](/en/kung-fu/wudang-mountain-sword/).

## The Hook Hand: Signature Technique

The most distinctive feature of Praying Mantis is the **hook hand** (勾手). Practitioners curl their fingers and wrist to mimic the mantis's forelegs, creating a tool that simultaneously blocks, grabs, and strikes. The hook hand catches incoming punches, redirects them, and instantly counters — all in one fluid motion.

Key techniques include:

- **Tanglang Buxue** (螳螂捕蝉) — "Mantis Catches Cicada": intercept and counter
- **Bengbu** (崩补) — "Crushing Step": explosive forward pressure
- **Jie Gou** (截勾) — "Intercepting Hook": parry and strike simultaneously

The style emphasizes continuous attack — a mantis does not retreat. Practitioners train to maintain relentless forward pressure, overwhelming opponents with rapid combinations.

## Fighting Strategy

Praying Mantis Kung Fu operates on three core principles: **speed over strength**, **angle over distance**, and **simultaneous defense and attack**. Unlike [Wing Chun](/en/kung-fu/wing-chun-centerline/), which focuses on the centerline, Mantis attacks from angles — circling, hooking, and striking from unexpected directions.

The footwork is distinctive: short, quick steps that keep the practitioner in optimal striking range. Low kicks target the opponent's legs to destabilize them, while the hands deliver rapid combinations to the head and torso.

## Modern Relevance

Today, Praying Mantis is practiced worldwide and remains a staple of Chinese wushu competitions. Its emphasis on practical efficiency has made it popular among MMA fighters exploring traditional Chinese striking arts.
