On July 15, 2004, FIFA President Sepp Blatter stood in Zibo, Shandong Province, and made a declaration that reshaped football history: **cuju** (蹴鞠) — not England's medieval folk football — was the earliest form of the world's most popular sport.

## A Game Born in Warring States

The word *cuju* combines two characters: *cu* (蹴), meaning "to kick," and *ju* (鞠), referring to a leather ball stuffed with hair or feathers. Historical records first mention the game during the [Warring States Period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warring_States_period) (475–221 BCE), when it served as military training for soldiers.

By the [Han Dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Han_dynasty) (206 BCE–220 CE), cuju had evolved into a formalized sport with dedicated courts. Emperor Wu of Han was such an enthusiast that he had cuju fields built inside the palace. The game had two forms: *Baida cuju* (白打蹴鞠), a solo performance style emphasizing juggling and acrobatic kicks, and *Zhuju cuju* (筑球蹴鞠), a competitive team match.

## The Golden Age: Song Dynasty

Cuju reached its zenith during the [Song Dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Song_dynasty) (960–1279 CE). Professional cuju clubs called *qiyunshe* (齐云社) — literally "cloud-reaching societies" — emerged, complete with written rules, rankings, and organized tournaments. This was centuries before any comparable football organization existed in Europe.

The Song-era ball was a remarkable feat of craftsmanship: 12 pieces of leather stitched over an inflated animal bladder, creating a near-perfect sphere. The *Qiyun Club Rules*, compiled around the 12th century, specified player positions, scoring systems, and even dress codes.

## How Cuju Was Played

In its competitive form, two teams faced each other across a field with a goal mounted high on a pole. Players passed and kicked the ball through the opening without using their hands — a rule that football would adopt centuries later. The solo performance form was arguably more spectacular: players juggled the ball with feet, knees, and shoulders in choreographed routines, some executing moves with names like "swallows returning to nest" and "lotuses moving in the wind."

The [Qingming Scroll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Along_the_River_During_the_Qingming_Festival), China's most famous panoramic painting, depicts cuju players in the streets of the Song capital Kaifeng.

## From Decline to Revival

Cuju declined during the [Qing Dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qing_dynasty) as the Manchu rulers preferred [ice skating](/en/games/chinese-diabolo-kongzhu/) and archery. By the 19th century, the game had nearly vanished. But in 2004, FIFA's official recognition sparked a revival. Zibo now hosts annual cuju festivals, and the game is taught in schools across Shandong Province as [intangible cultural heritage](https://ich.unesco.org/).

Today, a [vintage leather football](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0FN7J98BX?tag=huaxiaink-20) on your shelf is more than decor — it's a nod to the 2,000-year journey from a Warring States military drill to the beautiful game played by billions.
