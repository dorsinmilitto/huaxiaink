For three days each spring, ancient Chinese households extinguished every flame and ate only cold food. This was **Hanshi Festival** (寒食节) — the Cold Food Festival — a solemn observance that preceded [Qingming](/en/festivals/qingming-tomb-sweeping/) and eventually merged with it.

## The Legend of Jie Zitui

The festival traces to a tragic story from the [Spring and Autumn period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spring_and_Autumn_period) (771–476 BCE). Jie Zitui was a loyal retainer of Prince Chong'er of Jin. When the prince was exiled and starving, Jie cut flesh from his own thigh to feed him.

Years later, Prince Chong'er reclaimed his throne as Duke Wen of Jin. He rewarded all who helped him — except Jie, who had retired to Mount Mian with his mother, refusing any accolade. The duke, desperate to summon Jie, set the mountain on fire to force him out. Jie and his mother perished in the flames, clinging to a willow tree.

Overcome with remorse, Duke Wen ordered three days of no fire in Jie's memory. The cold food tradition was born.

## Three Days Without Fire

During Hanshi, households ate pre-cooked rice, cold vegetables, and preserved foods. No stoves burned, no candles lit. The ban was strict — in some periods, violators faced punishment.

The practical effect was a nationwide pause. Without cooking fires, communities turned to prepared foods: cold noodles, pickled vegetables, and *qingtuan* — the green glutinous rice dumplings now associated with [Qingming](/en/festivals/qingming-tomb-sweeping/). The festival served as a seasonal marker — the transition from winter's preserved foods to spring's fresh growth.

## The Poetry of Cold Food

The Tang dynasty poet Han Hong immortalized Hanshi in his famous poem "Cold Food Festival," describing spring scenes in the imperial palace:

> *Spring in the city, flying blossoms everywhere; / Cold food season, the east wind sweeps the willow branches awry.*

The poem subtly criticizes imperial privilege — palace candles given special exemption from the fire ban while commoners shivered in cold darkness.

## How Hanshi Merged With Qingming

Over centuries, the strict fire ban relaxed. Hanshi and Qingming fell on adjacent days (Hanshi on the 105th day after winter solstice, Qingming the next day). By the [Tang Dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tang_dynasty), the two had effectively merged into the single Qingming observance we know today.

The cold food tradition survives in the custom of eating *qingtuan* during Qingming and in the symbolic avoidance of cooking in some rural communities.
