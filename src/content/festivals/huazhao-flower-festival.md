On the 12th day of the second lunar month, ancient China threw a birthday party — not for a person, but for every flower in the land. This was **Huazhao Festival** (花朝节), the Flower Birthday Festival, and it remains one of China's most poetic celebrations.

## A Birthday for All Flowers

Huazhao literally means "flower morning." The festival marks the moment spring truly arrives — when plum blossoms fade and peach, cherry, and magnolia burst open across the countryside. Ancient Chinese considered this day the collective birthday of all flowers.

The tradition dates to the [Tang Dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tang_dynasty), when Emperor Xuanzong established official celebrations in Chang'an. Court ladies adorned their hair with fresh blossoms, poets composed verses beneath flowering trees, and the emperor himself distributed flower-themed gifts.

## The Flower Goddess

Central to Huazhao is the **Hua Shen** (花神) — the Flower Goddess. She is not a single deity but a pantheon: each month and each flower has its own goddess. The most revered is the goddess of plum blossoms, Mei Fei, symbolizing resilience in winter's end.

Temples held special ceremonies offering tea, wine, and fresh flowers to the goddesses. Participants tied red ribbons on flower branches — a tradition called *shanghua* (赏花), meaning "appreciating flowers" — to wish for good fortune and beauty in the coming year.

## The Hanfu Revival

In recent years, Huazhao has experienced a remarkable revival, driven by the [hanfu movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanfu_movement). Young Chinese don traditional silk robes and parade through parks and gardens in a stunning display of cultural pride.

Shanghai's annual Huazhao Festival draws thousands in hanfu, with flower markets, poetry competitions, and traditional music performances. The visual spectacle — streams of silk-clad figures drifting through cherry blossoms — has made it one of China's most photogenic festivals.

## Poetry and Petals

Huazhao has always been a literary festival. The great Tang poet Bai Juyi wrote:

> *On Flower Festival day, I drink alone beneath the blossoms; / Who will share this spring beauty with me?*

The tradition of writing poems on flower-shaped paper and hanging them from branches continues at modern Huazhao events.
